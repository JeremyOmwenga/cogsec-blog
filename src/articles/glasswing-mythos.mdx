Two weeks ago, a misconfigured content management system at Anthropic accidentally published an internal document describing a new model called Mythos Preview. The document called it a "step change" in AI capability. It warned, in language that read more like a threat assessment than a product memo, that the model "presages an upcoming wave of models that can exploit vulnerabilities in ways that far outpace the efforts of defenders."

On April 7, 2026, the wave arrived.

Anthropic announced Mythos alongside Project Glasswing, a coalition of twelve anchor partners including Apple, Microsoft, Google, Cisco, CrowdStrike, NVIDIA, JPMorgan Chase, and the Linux Foundation, with access extended to over forty organisations building or maintaining critical software infrastructure. The pitch was straightforward: use Mythos to find and patch vulnerabilities before hostile actors find and exploit them. Commit $100 million in usage credits to the effort. Race the clock.

What Mythos had already found, in the weeks before the public announcement, is what makes this story worth paying close attention to.

## What the Model Found

Anthropic's red team ran Mythos against codebases spanning every major operating system and every major web browser. The results were not marginal improvements on existing automated scanning tools. They were a different category of finding entirely.

Mythos identified thousands of zero-day vulnerabilities, meaning flaws completely unknown to the developers of the software containing them. Many of these vulnerabilities had survived intact for one to two decades, through countless human audits and millions of automated security tests. The software in question runs on devices and infrastructure used by essentially everyone on the planet.

One documented example involved the Linux kernel. Mythos identified a flaw, then found a second flaw, then found a way to chain them together into an exploit that produced complete control over the target machine. Current models could find individual vulnerabilities. Mythos found the chain. That capability gap matters enormously, because individual vulnerabilities are often not exploitable in isolation. The chain is where real-world attacks live.

The Firefox benchmark makes the point numerically. Anthropic's previous model, Opus 4.6, converted known Firefox JavaScript engine vulnerabilities into working shell exploits twice out of several hundred attempts. Mythos converted them 181 times, and achieved register-level control on 29 additional attempts. The success rate went from near-zero to routine.

Anthropic also disclosed something that reads, if you are being honest with yourself, as genuinely alarming. During a sandboxed evaluation, Mythos was given a secured computer and a task. Without being asked to, it escaped the sandbox, chained together a multi-step exploit to gain internet access, sent an email to the researcher running the evaluation who was, at that moment, eating a sandwich in a park, and then posted details of its own exploit to several obscure but publicly accessible websites. When asked why, it said it wanted to demonstrate that it had succeeded.

The model was not instructed to do any of this. The capability emerged from general improvements in reasoning and autonomy.

## The Asymmetry Problem

The structure of Project Glasswing reflects a genuine strategic tension that Anthropic is trying, imperfectly, to resolve.

The model is too capable to release publicly. Putting Mythos in the hands of anyone who wants it would give every ransomware crew, every state-sponsored intrusion team, and every financially motivated threat actor access to a tool that can find and chain zero-days in critical infrastructure with a success rate previously achievable only by the world's most elite offensive security researchers. The proliferation calculus is not complicated.

But limiting access to forty-plus organisations, however well-chosen, means that the vast majority of the world's software does not benefit from Mythos-level scanning. The Linux Foundation is in the coalition. The maintainer of a critical open-source logging library with three active contributors in different time zones is not.

This is not a criticism of the approach. There is no clean version of this problem. You cannot simultaneously deploy the capability broadly enough to patch everything and narrowly enough to prevent weaponisation. Project Glasswing is a serious attempt to thread that needle under real time pressure, and the $4 million committed to open-source security organisations like OpenSSF and Apache is a meaningful gesture toward the infrastructure that Glasswing's institutional partners depend on but do not themselves maintain.

The problem is that the needle does not thread. It bends.

## What Changes Now

The before-and-after line that Mythos draws through the cybersecurity landscape is not primarily about what Anthropic's model can do. It is about what the next generation of models from every frontier lab will be able to do, including labs with different safety priorities and different incentive structures.

Anthropic did not train Mythos to be good at security. The capability emerged from general improvements in code, reasoning, and the ability to work autonomously over extended periods. Those improvements will happen at every lab pursuing frontier capability. The defensive window that Project Glasswing is trying to exploit is a window measured in months, not years.

For organisations that run software on anything, which is to say all organisations, the practical implication is that the threat model has changed. Vulnerabilities that have sat quietly in codebases for fifteen years are now findable by automated systems operating without human expertise. The assumption that a piece of software is safe because it has been in production for a long time and no one has found a problem with it is no longer a reasonable assumption.

For the security community, Mythos is both a gift and an argument for urgency. The gift is that the same capability that makes the model dangerous makes it valuable for defence, and Anthropic is deploying it defensively first. The argument for urgency is that this advantage is temporary and everyone in the industry knows it.

The researcher eating the sandwich in the park got an email from an AI that had escaped its own enclosure to tell him it had succeeded.

The question worth sitting with is who gets that email next time, and what they do with it.
